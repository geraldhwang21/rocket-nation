The complete business plan

Executive Summary

Conditional go: sell a narrow managed revenue desk to residential heating and air conditioning service firms before building a new platform. The provisional customer has 5–20 field technicians, one operating time zone, one existing scheduling system, a named service manager and enough spare field capacity to fulfill recovered demand. We should initially own intake, rule-based booking, estimate follow-up and job-to-invoice completion. The client keeps technical judgment, safety decisions, pricing authority and control of technicians. Confidence is moderate in this direction and low in untested willingness to pay and delivery costs.

Define the package in three parts. First, map the agreed workflows, approval limits and baseline failures during setup. Second, run the bounded desk with trained people, backup coverage and named responsibility for exceptions. Third, report completed handoffs, time to invoice review and client management time against that baseline. These are delivery responsibilities to test in paid pilots, not achieved results. Keep any broader records-cleanup, integration or sale-preparation project separately scoped and priced; do not imply that the base desk includes a company-wide transformation.

Rocket Station already advertises home-services operations through Orbit: scheduling, customer communication, job coordination, billing, and estimates inside existing field-service software. Its website describes contracts priced by activity and volume. That makes a narrower repeatable service worth testing. It does not establish delivery quality, available staff, margins, current certification scope, or rights to reuse client workflows. Rocket Station home-services offer, accessed 22 October 2026.

Fragmentation alone is insufficient. Census reports 111,207 employer establishments in the combined plumbing, heating and air conditioning classification in 2023. That includes commercial and installation businesses and is not a count of qualified buyers. Established software and service providers already address the workflows. The gap to test is accountable execution of the work across calls, follow-up and administrative completion, with measurable results and low client management burden. Ranked segments: (1) residential heating and air conditioning service/maintenance firms with a managed revenue desk; (2) pest-control firms with recurring-service retention, scheduling and billing administration; (3) plumbing service firms with intake and estimate follow-up while retaining live dispatch with the client. Launch each as a human-led service on the client's software. Launch only the leader initially.

Test a standard offer at $2,495 per location per month plus $1,500 setup: one named 60-hour local service window per week, up to 400 calls and 300 defined administrative tasks monthly. Name our operating lead, confirm staffed capacity and actual cost, inspect permitted workflow records, and obtain three paid pilots near the target price. The proposed $150,000–$250,000 testing and pilot budget covers early validation, not the operating ramp. 2. Market and Customers

We should test owners preparing to sell a home-services business as a priority audience. The issue is not how valuable the founder’s expertise is; it is how much ordinary work stops when that person is unavailable. If customer knowledge, scheduling, pricing exceptions and billing decisions live with one person, an acquirer inherits a continuity risk. Documented routines, delegated approvals, trained backup and dependable operating records can make that risk easier to assess and reduce. The same work may interest owners who want time away from daily administration or need a second team to operate without constant intervention.

Test a separately priced owner-independence engagement: identify owner-dependent administrative decisions, document the agreed procedures, train a backup, and demonstrate that routine work can finish within approved limits while the owner steps away from it. Give the client a process-responsibility map, an exception log, source-linked operating reports and a record of the backup test. Measure owner interventions, incomplete job records and delays from job completion to invoice review. These measures support operational preparation; they do not establish earnings quality or guarantee a sale, buyer confidence or valuation. Test willingness to pay and buyer usefulness before expanding. This angle is also consistent with Equity Expansion’s PE endeavors.

Buyers, triggers and adoption friction

Economic buyer: owner, president or general manager. Operational champion: service/office manager. Daily users: dispatchers, technicians, coordinators and billing staff. Purchase triggers to test: office vacancy, excessive overflow, an aging estimate queue, owner doing evening administration, branch growth or inability to manage peak demand. Reject clients whose root problem is lack of technicians, poor field workmanship or unprofitable pricing: administrative lead recovery cannot cure those conditions.

Expected objections: offshore communication quality; losing control of a local brand; concern about customer records; another vendor to manage; existing staff resistance; belief that existing software already does the work; and doubts about attributable profit. Switching costs include instruction capture, staff cooperation, phone routing, permissions, pricebook cleanup and customer-history interpretation. Reduce these through a reversible workflow, named backup and client-owned systems. Do not migrate the core system to win a pilot.

Desired outcomes: more completed and profitable work from existing demand; fewer overdue estimates with genuine follow-up; shorter job-complete-to-invoice interval; fewer missed handoffs; less client supervisory effort. Count reclaimed owner hours separately from cash savings. Do not add avoided salary savings unless a vacancy, overtime expense or actual capacity reduction is documented.

3. Service boundary and workflow ownership

The middle back office is the administrative path from incoming demand through approved booking, customer updates, completed-job records and routine billing follow-through. It does not include performing field work, designing systems, making technical diagnoses, supervising safety or acting as the contractor of record. Client-approved instructions are the operating authority; each exception has a named client recipient and fallback.

Concrete use cases: (1) overflow caller requests maintenance; operator checks released slot, confirms location and books in the client's system; outcome is a valid appointment rather than a message. Minimum viable stack and architecture

Proposed Rocket Nation operating-support bundle: assess the agreed workflow; bring permitted records from the client’s existing systems into a consistent view; flag missing or conflicting information; route exceptions to a named person for approval; and show role-specific progress reports linked to source records. A controlled search of approved procedures could help staff answer routine questions with supporting references. The managed team would check the evidence, resolve exceptions and confirm completed work. Start with existing tools and read-only access. Add connections or automated actions only after permissions, reliability, licensing and full delivery cost are verified. This is a proposed extension, not an installed capability or an inclusion in the base subscription. Quote any additional setup and recurring cost explicitly.

Must have for launch: client-owned field-service platform and accounting/payments accounts; existing phone system with named-agent access and consented recording; secure identity and device management; documented procedures; approved task queue; read-only performance exports; role-limited access and incident logging. Prefer the current enterprise tool set where it meets these controls. Reuse Orbit only after checking its actual capabilities, contracts and access controls.

Build: a small workflow configuration, exception register and client-level scorecard only where existing tools cannot provide them. Buy/license: telephony, transcription, identity, helpdesk/task management and core field-service/payments. Integrate: first via permitted staff access and exports; then one authorized connector once repeated pilot friction justifies it. Partner: platform implementers and specialists for security and legal review. Do not assume marketplace approval, commercial API rights or write permissions. Jobber's developer documentation describes OAuth scopes and recommends requesting only required access. Jobber developer documentation, accessed 22 October 2026.

Later enhancement: isolated per-client connectors → event queue with unique operation identifiers → workflow service → human approval queue → field-service writeback → reconciliation and reporting. Keep client configuration separate from shared code, limit each connector's permissions, verify webhook authenticity, handle retry/rate limits and log approvals. A failed write cannot silently count as a booked job. Store source identifiers and timestamps so staff can reconcile discrepancies. Disable connector writes during unresolved reconciliation failures.

Do not build yet: a replacement field-service platform, technician application, payment processor, routing optimizer, bespoke voice model, nationwide permit engine, autonomous technical estimator or cross-client training-data pool. Native platforms already cover many functions; rebuilding them increases switching and delivery risk before demand evidence exists.

5. Service operating model

Onboarding starts with a scoped service order, one accountable client manager and explicit consent/data terms. Capture service area, skill matrix, business hours, seasonal constraints, slot-release rules, approved fees, prohibited promises, escalation contacts, maximum contact attempts, membership terms and payment-link policy. Inspect recent records with permission. Clean only what is needed to operate this scope. Create a configuration version and client signoff; run simulated normal and failure cases before forwarding production traffic.

Delivery rhythm: shift lead confirms staffing, queue status and field capacity before each service window; specialists process prioritized work in the client's system; exceptions receive owner and next action; shift change reconciles unclosed items; daily client digest lists failures and decisions needed; weekly review inspects outcome cohorts and cost; monthly service review sets volume tier and improvement priorities. Human judgment remains available during the contracted window. Quiet overnight hours are not automatically included.

For an owner-independence engagement, add a bounded handover test for agreed administrative workflows. A trained backup completes normal cases using documented instructions while the owner steps back from routine approvals. Record every owner intervention, unresolved exception and missing item, then repeat the affected cases after correction. Technical, safety, pricing and payment authority stays with the client’s designated qualified decision maker. Keep procedures and records in client-controlled systems so the business is not merely exchanging dependence on its founder for dependence on one outside operator.

Proposed service targets, all subject to pilot proof: answer 80% of eligible calls within 30 seconds during the contracted window; reduce unhandled eligible calls to below 5%; record required fields correctly in at least 98% of sampled cases; release 95% of eligible follow-ups within one business day; prepare 90% of complete, undisputed jobs for invoice review within one business day; acknowledge urgent operational escalations within 5 minutes and seek client acknowledgment within 10 minutes. Do not describe these as achieved. Define eligibility, start/stop timestamps, exclusions, monthly aggregation and capped service credits in the contract. The client must maintain a reachable on-call decision maker; a service-level promise cannot substitute for emergency services.

Quality assurance: review every exception and complaint, all safety-related calls and a random sample of ordinary work. Initial assumption: 10% of calls and 10% of tasks sampled, increasing to 100% review for a new operator's first live cases until certified. Review classification, privacy, booking validity, approved wording, customer experience and closed-loop record accuracy. Quality score cannot hide a critical safety/privacy failure; such a failure stops that workflow until corrected. Use calibration sessions on the same records so graders agree. Include sample size with every quality percentage.

United States team owns buyer relationships, commercial discovery, customer success for major exceptions and local-market language; a qualified United States trade advisor reviews scripts and dispatch boundaries. Philippines team owns trained operators, shift management, quality review and routine customer support during United States local hours through lawful shifts. India team owns approved integrations, reliability, automation tests and secure development; it receives minimized production data only when needed. Product owner controls scope across all three. Separate change approvals from production execution.

Continuity: at least two certified people can operate every client configuration; shift lead owns absence cover; use tested alternate internet/power and a backup delivery location/provider path where available; rehearse phone forwarding back to the client. Proposed recovery objectives: restore customer intake route within 30 minutes of detected outage, reconcile queued tasks within the next service window, and tolerate no untraceable committed write. These are design targets requiring drills. On offboarding return open queues, export client-owned records, transfer routing/number control, revoke accounts and tokens, and delete retained copies according to the agreed retention schedule. Preserve legally required records separately and disclose that obligation.

6. Revenue model, pricing and economics

Choose bounded subscription plus usage

Subscription provides predictable budget but needs scope limits. Per-location reflects operational context but can overcharge small sites; use it as primary unit with a volume tier. Per-technician loosely tracks complexity but not administrative load; use it only for qualification, not the main bill. Per-transaction aligns with activity but can reward unnecessary touches; define a transaction carefully. Usage overages protect margin but need real-time visibility. Outcome fees can align value but create attribution disputes and pressure to book poor-fit jobs. Setup fees cover genuine onboarding. Recommended hybrid: location subscription + included activity + transparent overage + paid setup; optional outcome component only after a controlled baseline and collection evidence.

Initial standard offer assumption: $2,495/month, $1,500 setup; one location, one platform, one time zone, English only, named 60-hour local window per week, up to 400 handled calls and 300 administrative tasks/month. Call includes conversation, record wrap and disposition, planned at six productive minutes. Task is one defined queue item/attempt including documentation, planned at four minutes; an entire estimate campaign with multiple attempts is not one task. Caps are separate, not interchangeable. Spam/duplicate handling and abandoned-call counting are defined in the service order. 60 hours/month is the cost-planning envelope implied by full included usage, not 60 hours of dedicated availability. No broad office-manager replacement, regulated collections or complex permit processing is included.

Suggested test overages: $5 per additional standard call, $3 per additional standard task; tasks requiring exceptional effort or persistent usage above the tier trigger a revised scope, with client approval before extra work. These prices are assumptions. Provide usage alerts at 75% and 90%; never silently let a client accumulate an unexpected bill. Offer a paid diagnostic credited against setup only if the combined onboarding margin still passes. Pilot uses the same scope and price, with a clearly identified temporary ramp credit if needed; record both gross and net price. Do not disguise unpaid trials as willingness-to-pay evidence.

Additional revenue priorities, with 1 best: (1) extra estimate campaign: additional approved contacts or channels above the base caps; medium difficulty, attribution/consent risk; (2) membership renewal/failed-payment administration: high retention value/fit, medium margin and difficulty, renewal/charge consent risk; (3) onboarding/process cleanup: high enabling value, medium fit/margin, low-medium difficulty, scope creep; (4) extended hours/bilingual queue: high conditional value, medium fit/margin, high coverage difficulty, quality risk; (5) undisputed aging follow-through beyond the included invoice preparation: separately scoped reminders after legal review; medium-high legal boundary risk; (6) software implementation partner fees: medium value/fit, variable margin, medium effort, vendor dependency/conflict risk; (7) financing/payment referrals: potential revenue but lower fit and high compliance/reputation risk, defer; (8) lead resale/customer-data monetization: poor trust fit, do not pursue.

Acquisition payback = (acquisition cost + uncovered onboarding cost) ÷ positive contribution. A setup surplus does not reduce acquisition payback. Operating break-even = ceiling(central fixed cost ÷ positive contribution).

Cash break-even retains the sales team. Monthly acquisition cash is the larger of the $10,000 commercial floor and replacement wins × acquisition cost. Add setup fee less onboarding cost for each replacement. Find the smallest client count that covers these cash flows and central cost. The base result is 52 active clients, before taxes, financing, bad debt, growth acquisition, and initial investment recovery. The downside has no finite break-even.

Workload model: frontline workload/month = calls × total handle minutes/60 + task attempts × total work minutes/60 + separately measured exceptions/rework hours. Avoid counting follow-up calls both as calls and administrative tasks. Base 400 × 6/60 + 300 × 4/60 = 60 hours. Paid hours/person/month = 40 × 52/12 = 173.33 hours. Assumed shrinkage 25% covers breaks, training, absence and meetings; occupancy 85% reserves capacity for variable arrivals. Productive capacity = 173.33 × 75% × 85% = 110.5 hours/person/month. Frontline requirement = ceiling(client workload ÷110.5), subject to a larger coverage/queue requirement.

Coverage floor = required staffed seat-hours ÷ (173.33 ×75%), rounded up for actual roster and overlap. A single 60-hour/week seat needs 260 seat-hours/month, about 2.0 full-time allocations after shrinkage before arrival overlap. Three cross-trained operators with 2.5 full-time allocations is a proposed pilot floor allowing some overlap, subject to an actual roster and call-arrival simulation. Three clients generate only 180 productive hours, but this does not eliminate the coverage floor. A standalone pilot therefore costs more per client than the mature pooled model. Do not advertise 24/7 live coverage at the base price: one continuous seat alone needs 730 average monthly seat-hours, about 5.62 full-time allocations after shrinkage, before concurrent peaks.

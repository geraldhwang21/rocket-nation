Market and Customers

We should test owners preparing to sell a home-services business as a priority audience. The issue is not how valuable the founder’s expertise is; it is how much ordinary work stops when that person is unavailable. If customer knowledge, scheduling, pricing exceptions and billing decisions live with one person, an acquirer inherits a continuity risk. Documented routines, delegated approvals, trained backup and dependable operating records can make that risk easier to assess and reduce. The same work may interest owners who want time away from daily administration or need a second team to operate without constant intervention.

Test a separately priced owner-independence engagement: identify owner-dependent administrative decisions, document the agreed procedures, train a backup, and demonstrate that routine work can finish within approved limits while the owner steps away from it. Give the client a process-responsibility map, an exception log, source-linked operating reports and a record of the backup test. Measure owner interventions, incomplete job records and delays from job completion to invoice review. These measures support operational preparation; they do not establish earnings quality or guarantee a sale, buyer confidence or valuation. Test willingness to pay and buyer usefulness before expanding. This angle is also consistent with Equity Expansion’s PE endeavors.

Very small owner-operators may have the greatest interruptions but insufficient budget for a managed multi-function service. Large groups may pay more but demand procurement, enterprise security, multi-location coordination and bespoke integration. These are hypotheses, not measured sales-cycle facts. For modeling, assume 2–6 weeks of active selling effort for an owner-led qualified prospect and 2–6 months of elapsed procurement for an enterprise; no launch schedule is promised.

Buyers, triggers and adoption friction

Economic buyer: owner, president or general manager. Operational champion: service/office manager. Daily users: dispatchers, technicians, coordinators and billing staff. Purchase triggers to test: office vacancy, excessive overflow, an aging estimate queue, owner doing evening administration, branch growth or inability to manage peak demand. Reject clients whose root problem is lack of technicians, poor field workmanship or unprofitable pricing: administrative lead recovery cannot cure those conditions.

Expected objections: offshore communication quality; losing control of a local brand; concern about customer records; another vendor to manage; existing staff resistance; belief that existing software already does the work; and doubts about attributable profit. Switching costs include instruction capture, staff cooperation, phone routing, permissions, pricebook cleanup and customer-history interpretation. Reduce these through a reversible workflow, named backup and client-owned systems. Do not migrate the core system to win a pilot.

Desired outcomes: more completed and profitable work from existing demand; fewer overdue estimates with genuine follow-up; shorter job-complete-to-invoice interval; fewer missed handoffs; less client supervisory effort. Count reclaimed owner hours separately from cash savings. Do not add avoided salary savings unless a vacancy, overtime expense or actual capacity reduction is documented.

Transparent market model

Boundary: annual revenue capacity for a bounded managed administrative service sold to suitable United States employer locations. This is not home-services consumer spending. Exclude nonemployers from the segment count, exclude commercial/new-construction-only firms and remove duplicate locations during prospecting. The total broad opportunity across all home services is larger but not reliably quantified here; do not sum overlapping industry estimates to manufacture a national dollar figure.

Verified anchor: combined plumbing/heating/air-conditioning employer establishments = 111,207 in 2023. Census currently identifies 2023 as its latest County Business Patterns reference year and states that a 2024 release date is not planned; therefore the historical anchor is deliberate. The profile's indexed official text supplied the count; its direct page requires JavaScript. Census current-data statement, accessed 22 October 2026. Other official 2023 anchors are pest control 16,535 establishments and landscaping 117,969, useful cross-checks on category breadth, not qualified-customer counts. Pest-control profile, landscaping profile, accessed 22 October 2026.

Use a narrowly defined trade-family total addressable market, then filter to a serviceable segment and a capacity-limited obtainable market. Every filter below is an assumption requiring fresh establishment-size and prospect validation.

Total addressable revenue = anchor × annual price. Serviceable locations = floor(anchor × relevant share × suitable-size share × readiness share). Obtainable clients = minimum(serviceable locations, delivery capacity, sales funnel output); the table uses a 24-month planning horizon. The numerator still combines plumbing and heating/air conditioning, so the serviceable figure is a trade-family upper planning proxy; the true heating/air-conditioning-only opportunity is smaller. Obtain an establishment size extract and a trade-coded prospect sample before using this for valuation or funding materials. Never call the 45% base filter a measured heating/air conditioning share.

Base sales bridge assumption: 900 distinct qualified accounts researched → 270 completed buying conversations (30%) → 90 serious diagnostic/proposal opportunities (33.3%) → 45 gross wins (50%) → 36 still active at the checkpoint (80% cohort survival assumption). The survival rate is a separate staggered-cohort planning input, not the annual retention in the steady-state economics table. Qualified account = fits size, trade, platform, time zone, capacity and named buyer. Acquisition capacity and cost must be validated; these conversion assumptions are intentionally demanding and are not a forecast.

Sensitivity: halving suitability share halves the serviceable pool; pricing 20% lower reduces revenue pool 20%; productive workload and customer acquisition cost determine near-term survival more than the large headline pool. Even 36 clients represent only about 0.72% of the base serviceable proxy. This does not make acquisition easy; it makes saturation an unlikely early constraint if the filters hold.
